Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they support roughly a quarter of all known marine species. A reef is built by tiny animals called polyps, which draw calcium and carbonate from seawater and turn them into hard limestone skeletons. Over thousands of years, these skeletons pile up into the massive structures that divers recognize today. Most reef-building corals depend on microscopic algae living inside their tissues: the algae supply food through photosynthesis, and the coral provides shelter in return. When the water stays too warm for too long, corals expel the algae and turn white, a process known as bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead, but it is starving, and repeated heat waves leave little time for recovery. Scientists are now testing heat-tolerant coral strains and replanting damaged areas, while stressing that cutting pollution and limiting ocean warming remain the most important long-term protections.
